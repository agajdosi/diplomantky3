<h1>B&Aacute;RA GEHRINGEROV&Aacute;</h1>
<h2>Where Human Touches the Earth</h2>
<p><em>Painting Studio 1</em><br><br><strong>The motif of touching the landscape recurs frequently in your work. I am&nbsp;curious about the way you think about this idea. Is it a subtle way of establishing a connection, or does touching the landscape already entail a specific intervention or transformation? It seems to me that the landscape is&nbsp;not just an environment for you but also a partner in dialogue.</strong><br>I consider a person coming in touch with the landscape as a moment of&nbsp;encounter between two worlds. It is a small gesture of engagement that generates closeness, curiosity, and the possibility of transformation and power.&nbsp;My paintings therefore work with the ambivalence of human presence in the&nbsp;landscape. Here, the human is understood neither as purely destructive nor&nbsp;purely protective, but rather as one side of a dialogue within a process of&nbsp;discovery and the search for mutual boundaries. The landscape is a space&nbsp;that accepts human presence, bears its traces, and at the same time preserves its own autonomy.</p>
<p><strong>Where do you think the moment of transition from being an observer of&nbsp;the landscape to an &ldquo;owner&rdquo; of the space occurs? Can the very desire for&nbsp;a home in the landscape lead to its subtle transformation?</strong><br>As part of the paintings, the moment of transition from being an observer to&nbsp;becoming a person who makes the space their own occurs when the figure&nbsp;is no longer merely passing through but decides to remain in the landscape.&nbsp;The longing for a home likely also carries with it the need to shape, protect,&nbsp;and define the space. Although the images explore the romantic theme of&nbsp;the search for a home and respect for the landscape, there are also gradual&nbsp;changes that are taking place, which irrevocably transform its character. This&nbsp;motif of transformation is also reflected in the pyrography technique I use&nbsp;throughout the series: the wood physically changes under the soldering iron,&nbsp;and the image is created through irreversible intervention in the material&nbsp;itself. For me, the slow process of creation thus represents a parallel to&nbsp;human activity in the landscape.</p>
<p><strong>As a form of conclusion, you raise the question of how we will inhabit&nbsp;the landscape in the future &ndash; whether as a space we will dominate, or as&nbsp;a place with which we will form a close relationship. I cannot help but ask:&nbsp;do you think humans are still capable of perceiving the landscape beyond&nbsp;the context of ownership and exploitation?</strong><br>As part of my work, I primarily focus on the individual&rsquo;s personal relationship&nbsp;to the landscape. I believe that it is precisely on this level that it is possible to understand the landscape in a more natural way, rather than from&nbsp;a general, distant and global perspective. But I still do not have an answer&nbsp;to this question.</p>
