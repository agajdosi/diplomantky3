<h1>JAMIE HRABCŮ</h1>
<h2>Overall</h2>
<p><em>Photography Studio</em><br><br><strong>I am curious about your approach to stained glass. Do you consider it&nbsp;a metaphor for mending broken bonds, or is it rather about acknowledging that relationships cannot be the same as they were before a certain&nbsp;turning point?</strong><br>A bit of both. Although for me, stained glass is not primarily about restoring&nbsp;things to their original form, but rather about connecting seemingly disparate&nbsp;elements. Every shard of glass is unique in its own way and shape, and only&nbsp;through focused care &ndash; further breaking, grinding, and minor adjustments &ndash; is&nbsp;it possible to fit them back together and create the final whole. I consider&nbsp;it an acknowledgment of the existence of diversity and, at the same time,&nbsp;an homage to it.</p>
<p><strong>Your work uses the motif of protective elements, such as sliders, spine&nbsp;protectors, and the like. How do you think the meaning of protection&nbsp;changes when you transform their material into fragile materials such&nbsp;as glass or wax?</strong><br>I love those moments when the original purpose of things is disrupted, their&nbsp;nature is called into question, and their meaning is reinterpreted. What does&nbsp;a particular object actually mean, what does it represent? A glass spine&nbsp;protector loses its function, yet at the same time it finds meaning in establishing connection, regardless of the cracks, and acknowledges vulnerability.&nbsp;<br>The original function of the sliders is not to protect against impact, but to&nbsp;signal the lean angle to the riders. By deliberately scraping the slider against&nbsp;the asphalt while turning a corner, it monitors traction and prevents a fall. In<br>this work, I have made them of wax, as wax is the ideal material for this &ndash; its&nbsp;destruction through melting gives the material new form and shape.</p>
<p><strong>Your text often touches on the tension between freedom and discipline, not&nbsp;only in relationships but also in the art of motorcycle riding. How exactly&nbsp;do these aspects relate to you?</strong><br>A good motorcycle school teaches you that, on the road, you are the more&nbsp;vulnerable party, even if you&rsquo;re riding a powerful machine. That is why you&nbsp;must anticipate what happens around you and adapt to it. In relationships,&nbsp;we learn that it is good to be empathetic and sensitive, while still maintaining healthy boundaries.<br>Discipline and freedom do not have to be two opposing concepts; they&nbsp;go hand in hand. It is about listening to the situation, being mindful, and&nbsp;respecting others as well as yourself. Greater confidence &ndash; whether stemming from knowing the rules of the road and the terrain&rsquo;s possibilities, or&nbsp;the dynamics of relationships and our own emotions &ndash; allows us to relax.&nbsp;And the most fun part is treating everything as a process, a practice run,&nbsp;a leisurely ride to nowhere.</p>
