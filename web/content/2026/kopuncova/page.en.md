<h1>LIBU&Scaron;E KOPŮNCOV&Aacute;</h1>
<h2>Flesh in the Cloud</h2>
<p><em>Painting Studio 3</em><br><br><strong>In your work, you write that virtual performance is an experience that&nbsp;lies somewhere between a liminal experience and a &ldquo;technologically&nbsp;constructed hallucination of the real.&rdquo; Where do you think the boundary&nbsp;lies between a truly physical experience and its simulation in digital space?&nbsp;And is the &ldquo;frantic production of corporeality&rdquo; you discuss in your work&nbsp;more of a critique of digital culture (in its drive to replace physical experience) or do you see it as an opportunity to think about the new types of&nbsp;corporeality emerging in the digital environment?</strong><br>For me, these are not two separate things, but rather two layers of a single contemporary experience. I am interested in that paradoxical moment when the digital environment tries to mimic corporeality so strongly that it reveals what is missing from the body; the closer it gets, the further it drifts away. And I am not sure if we really want to bring the body back into digital space, or just fill the void left by its absence. I see the frantic production of corporeality as both a critique and a tool &ndash; it highlights this&nbsp;effort, but at the same time opens up the possibility of thinking about new&nbsp;types of corporeality. Moreover, I believe that the digital and physical no&nbsp;longer have fixed boundaries.</p>
<p><strong>Don&rsquo;t you think that viewing VR as a simulacrum that no longer represents&nbsp;reality but creates its own hyperreality runs the risk that a performance&nbsp;taking place in digital space will become an aesthetic image rather than&nbsp;a genuine shared experience?</strong><br>The risk that a performance will become just an image is real. But I try to&nbsp;avoid that by using slowness, silence, and ambiguity, which engage the&nbsp;viewer differently than visual attractors. I am not interested in an illusion&nbsp;of reality, but in a situation where the viewer begins to become more aware&nbsp;of themselves. I am interested in the moment when materiality recedes,&nbsp;leaving only its trace, so that the viewer remains somewhat removed.&nbsp;I believe it is precisely through this tension that an image can transform&nbsp;into an experience.&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Each act of the performance shows recurring themes, such as revelation, sacrifice or bodily transformation. How do you understand these rituals? And do you think they represent a path to inner transformation?&nbsp;</strong><br>I see rituals as a way to get closer to what we don&rsquo;t usually realize, to our memory and to inner states. It is not about a story, but a process: revelation, loss, and transformation. That transformation is not so much a result as a moment when something becomes present to us. I have no control over it, but I think one can take away a certain relational framework, a way of perceiving oneself and one&rsquo;s surroundings. And this individual experience can then be projected into a broader, collective context.</p>
