<h1>NAT&Aacute;LIE KLIM&Scaron;OV&Aacute;</h1>
<h2 class="p1">The Search for Water</h2>
<p><em>Painting Studio 1</em><br><br><strong>Water serves as a central motif in your series, yet it does not appear&nbsp;directly in the images. What does its visual absence mean to you?</strong><br>I understand water metaphorically as a kind of inner source or an indefinable&nbsp;prerequisite for survival. Since I partly perceive the thesis topic as a search&nbsp;and a journey, I also think of water as something inaccessible &ndash; perhaps&nbsp;as a goal that cannot be reached. I don&rsquo;t see this as a negative outcome,&nbsp;but rather as a legitimate part of the process. It is about the possibility of&nbsp;straying from the path, going off the beaten track, and redefining the goal&nbsp;during the journey.</p>
<p><strong>It strikes me that the castle, the pit, and water are not just motifs, but&nbsp;perhaps also certain psychological states. Do you think of the images as&nbsp;mental landscapes as well?</strong><br>I suppose I do. I often work with fairly general representations of places&nbsp;or objects, and I also return to certain motifs. I like mirrors, labyrinths, and&nbsp;gardens, for example. Although they don&rsquo;t always appear in the paintings&nbsp;directly, they operate somewhere in the background &ndash; and perhaps in my own&nbsp;imagination and thinking. These motifs carry a certain experience of space,&nbsp;yet they are not entirely concrete. I do not think of them strictly symbolically&nbsp;but rather in terms of the impression or state they can evoke or convey.</p>
<p><strong>Could you elaborate on what the I Ching system means to you and how it&nbsp;specifically manifests in your practice? Do you use it more as a symbolic&nbsp;and interpretive framework, a source of associations and structures, or&nbsp;do you also use it to make decisions during the actual painting process?</strong><br>For me, the <em>I Ching</em> serves primarily as a framework for interpretation and&nbsp;association. I don&rsquo;t use it directly as a decision-making tool while painting,&nbsp;but I think it has left its mark on my work nonetheless, especially in visual&nbsp;terms. Looking back at my Bachelor&rsquo;s series Garden, I realized that it was&nbsp;a period when I was most interested in the <em>I Ching</em>, and its principles also&nbsp;found their way into the paintings&rsquo; stylization. The system of lines in the&nbsp;<em>I Ching</em> is often described as providing certain pauses in the flow of time,&nbsp;known as the &ldquo;changes.&rdquo; I feel that some of that simplicity and the significance of the line have carried over into the way I construct and present&nbsp;my compositions. These are often simplified, sometimes composed of just&nbsp;a few lines, like scattered sticks.</p>
