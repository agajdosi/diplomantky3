<h1>EVA VAŇOV&Aacute;</h1>
<h2>The Aesthetics of the Presence</h2>
<p><em>Environment Studio</em><br><br><strong>Participatory practice is a key concept in your work. What is your previous&nbsp;experience with participative approaches and what communities do you&nbsp;usually work with?</strong><br>I have been engaged in participatory practice for about six years and have&nbsp;collaborated with various groups. Working with children became natural for&nbsp;me &ndash; childhood significantly shapes our later experiences, relationships, and&nbsp;how we approach ourselves, others, and our environment. I have worked with&nbsp;children from children&rsquo;s homes, children with autism or hearing impairments, the&nbsp;Roma and Ukrainian communities in community and social centers, as well as&nbsp;special schools. I have also done participatory work with adults in Ko&scaron;ice prison.</p>
<p><strong>Your work consists of an installation featuring embroidered images and carved&nbsp;wooden blocks. How do these relate to the participatory aspect of your work?</strong><br>My graduate work essentially reflects on my practice in participatory art, particularly my work with children from socially disadvantaged backgrounds. I have been trying to translate the live participatory process into a gallery setting without it becoming merely a form of documentation. That is why I address the principles of participation and the way they have influenced my own approach and visual language.<br>The work translates these principles into four fictional beings, each representing one aspect of the participatory process &ndash; physicality, translation of meaning,&nbsp;collective relationality, and the ethical awareness of boundaries. I materialized&nbsp;these beings as four types of wooden cubes.<br>Embroidered images then represent a more personal reflection on my participative projects. I primarily used felt, drawing, and embroidery thread which&nbsp;I perceive as layers of bodily memory, spontaneous emotional records, but<br>also as structures or boundaries that organize and limit space and experience.&nbsp;The installation explores how experiences, relationships, and emotions can turn&nbsp;into an image, an object, or a space that remains open to further interpretation&nbsp;and interaction.</p>
<p><strong>Who is the ideal audience for your work? Do you think your methods can be&nbsp;useful to educators, for example?</strong><br>I don&rsquo;t think my work has a single ideal audience &ndash; quite the opposite. For some,&nbsp;the exhibition may be primarily a visual experience. A social worker might&nbsp;see it as a way to work with safe spaces and communication; an educator&nbsp;may see it as an alternative, dialogic, and more sensitive form of education;&nbsp;and an artist may see it as an opportunity for participatory work. A child can&nbsp;engage with the work naturally through play, touch, and manipulating the&nbsp;blocks. But it is important that I don&rsquo;t view these methods as separate from&nbsp;art. Even though they touch on pedagogy or social work, they remain part of&nbsp;my artistic practice and my creative way of thinking.</p>
