<h1>VIKTOR MIKUS</h1>
<h2>You Only Die Once</h2>
<p><em>Game Media Studio</em><br><br><strong>Your graduate project focuses on the topic of dying in games. It is fascinating that death, which we perceive as so final in real life and whose&nbsp;mystery and irreversibility we are forced to deal with throughout our&nbsp;lives, can be experienced and inflicted practically endlessly in computer&nbsp;games. What did you discover about our relationship to death in computer games during your research?</strong><br>I was most interested in the concept of dual consciousness, described by&nbsp;Renata Ntelia: as a player, you identify with your avatar, but after its death,&nbsp;this connected consciousness is split &ndash; you remember the ending, but the&nbsp;avatar returns to the game pure, as if nothing had happened. Death in a game&nbsp;is therefore not death, but training.</p>
<p><strong>You have created an experimental controller-suit for your game. How does&nbsp;it function? What game mechanics did you work with? And how does multiplayer work in your game?</strong><br>The suit is not only a controller but also a bridge between the virtual and&nbsp;physical world, which is one of the main motifs of my work. The player puts&nbsp;on a mountaineering jacket which directly refers to the game environment,&nbsp;i.e. the high mountain terrain. There is a sensor hidden in the jacket that&nbsp;detects the player&rsquo;s breathing. This corresponds to the breathing of the&nbsp;avatar, which only has a limited amount of oxygen. So the faster the player&nbsp;breathes, the sooner his avatar dies. At the same time, they can see the&nbsp;curve of their breath in the user interface, while during testing the players&nbsp;subconsciously slowed down their breathing. In a certain way, the game&nbsp;goes beyond its own boundaries and affects the physiology of the player&nbsp;standing outside the game system.<br>Multiplayer works asynchronously. Players do not play simultaneously,&nbsp;but sequentially. After the death of the first player, another follows in his&nbsp;footsteps until he also dies, and so on.</p>
<p><strong>What kind of distribution or presentation format do you think is best for&nbsp;experimental games like this? Do you think that the exhibition is a suitable&nbsp;format for them, or can you imagine that people could buy your game to&nbsp;take home?</strong><br>Since the game is tied to a physical controller, an online presentation is&nbsp;out of the question. At the same time, it requires several players who take&nbsp;turns, not all simultaneously. It would be difficult for a person to take the&nbsp;suit home, since their death should not leave a legacy for anyone (laughs).&nbsp;It clearly belongs in the gallery.</p>
