<h1>CLARA GOLDENSTEIN</h1>
<h2><br>Further East, as Dusk Falls</h2>
<p><em>Fine Art and Design</em></p>
<p><strong>I feel like collecting is not only a working method for you, but a way of relating to the world. How do you think about the memory of found materials and images and the new meanings they can acquire once detached from&nbsp;their original context?</strong><br>I believe objects carry some kind of aura within them. That&rsquo;s why we are&nbsp;attracted to some and repelled by others&hellip; By &ldquo;aura&rdquo; or &ldquo;essence&rdquo; I mean&nbsp;traces of previous stories, routes, or environments, which become apparent&nbsp;through materiality &ndash; this is the most interesting part for me. Instead of&nbsp;buying a new piece of wood, working with a found one provides me with&nbsp;a multilayered starting point. It is already communicating something to me,&nbsp;and my work comes as a response to these reminiscences, adding layers&nbsp;that come from myself. The piece becomes a dialogue between me and the&nbsp;memories brought together with that surface. We work together.</p>
<p><strong>You work with the idea of identity as something constructed by fragments&nbsp;and associations. How do you understand this fragmentation in relation&nbsp;to the way identity functions today?</strong><br>I guess fragmentation is inherent today. Information comes from far away&nbsp;and also from up close. So I think that one way of building an identity within&nbsp;this fragmented reality is to incorporate this structure and play with it.&nbsp;Playing with association is kind of a natural response to that &ndash; we associate&nbsp;in order to create meaning and digest this huge amount of crumbled information. At the same time, there should be some space for grounding, for&nbsp;connecting with something that touches you deeply and clicks with a part of&nbsp;your identity. So, I understand this methodology as a process of finding what&nbsp;&ldquo;sticks&rdquo; to you within this fragmented, overwhelming reality, and creating&nbsp;some kind of &ldquo;auto-narrative.&rdquo;</p>
<p><strong>Collecting seems a kind of comforting activity for you. Does the process&nbsp;of searching and collecting sometimes become more important than the&nbsp;final object itself?</strong><br>Through searching and collecting I understand myself more &ndash; which is&nbsp;very comforting. For example, by coming to Central and Eastern Europe&nbsp;I found a part that shapes my identity deeply. It was initially overwhelming&nbsp;because I couldn&rsquo;t pinpoint the exact feeling. But then I slowly filled the&nbsp;walls and shelves of my bedroom, the metal boxes and folders from my&nbsp;studio &ndash; I created an inventory. This was part of my workflow, but also part&nbsp;of this identity-building process that can be extremely ambiguous: com&nbsp;forting but also a bit scary. Searching for objects in flea markets guides&nbsp;me through an intimate process of discovery, as the objects reveal the link&nbsp;between my identity and this land. To find the same blue glass vase from&nbsp;my grandma&rsquo;s house in Rio de Janeiro in a second-hand shop in Brno &ndash; what&nbsp;a weirdly beautiful vertigo&hellip;</p>
