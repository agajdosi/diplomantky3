<h1>MIKUL&Aacute;&Scaron; MITANA</h1>
<h2>Proxy</h2>
<p><em>Spatial Design Studio</em><br><br><strong>In your project, you used AI (ChatGPT) to create instructions for producing the final artwork. How did the process unfold, and what does the final&nbsp;set of instructions look like?</strong><br>I began by creating a prompt that would have the AI write a truly usable manual&nbsp;for the physical realization of a sculpture &ndash; not a description of its shape, but&nbsp;real instructions on how to create it. This turned out to be the main problem.&nbsp;The AI could generate texts that looked like instructions, but they were&nbsp;too ambiguous. Phrases like &ldquo;work with tension&rdquo; or &ldquo;leave space for quiet&rdquo;&nbsp;created a framework for thinking but didn&rsquo;t address specific questions.&nbsp;I revised the prompt over 11 versions. The turning point was manual number&nbsp;6, which the AI structured like a musical score with tempo markings. The&nbsp;resulting objects were created based on this one.<br>But the final assignment arose from the clash between text and material. And that is the main outcome of the work: instructions can develop a possible framework, but a sculpture only comes into being through physical decision-making. I drew primarily on my experience with metal work and welding.</p>
<p><strong>This reminded me of conceptual artists from the 1970s who simply wrote&nbsp;down instructions and anyone could theoretically create the work. What&nbsp;was the collaboration like with the non-human actor of artificial intelligence? Did you understand each other?</strong><br>The parallel with Sol LeWitt is accurate. But I tried to flip the situation. It wasn&rsquo;t&nbsp;that I, the artist, delegated the execution to others, but rather that the system&nbsp;delegated it to me. As the producer, I essentially swapped roles with the AI.&nbsp;<br>And I think we understood each other &ndash; or at least the AI tried to. But the&nbsp;process was more complex: I created prompts for it, and it defined rules&nbsp;for my work, which is also a kind of prompt. I realized AI is a dataset like any&nbsp;other &ndash; it reacts to what it is given. So I began working differently for later&nbsp;manuals: instead of general descriptions, I gave specific artists as reference&nbsp;points. This network of limits ensured the responses were not too vague.&nbsp;<br>It made sense when you knew the whole story of the prompts and the manuals. For someone without this background, it was confusing &ndash; instructions&nbsp;without context don&rsquo;t work.</p>
<p><strong>Was it possible to follow the AI&rsquo;s instructions step by step, or did you encounter any obstacles or paradoxes that forced you to deviate from them?</strong><br>The manual was so vague that I didn&rsquo;t know where to start. Then I started&nbsp;running out of time, so I decided to take the latest manual and just get&nbsp;started. I followed the rules the AI set for me and created the object in about&nbsp;4 hours. It was a matter of fairly random decision-making, but somehow it&nbsp;all fell into place. The main obstacle was that openness &ndash; there were no&nbsp;fixed points to build on.</p>
