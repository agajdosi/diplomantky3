<h1>AD&Eacute;LA KONEČN&Aacute;</h1>
<h2>Overload</h2>
<p><em>Video Studio</em><br><br><strong>In what way do you think a place can leave its mark on music with-&nbsp;out becoming immediately recognizable? And conversely, can music&nbsp;sometimes provide a more accurate interpretation of a place than the&nbsp;recordings themselves?</strong><br>It depends on how I feel in a particular place and whether I have any connection to it or memories of it. Just like music, certain places can stimulate&nbsp;various moods and states in me, whether pleasant, intense or unsettling.&nbsp;And I often find it easier to interpret these through music than through the&nbsp;field recordings themselves.</p>
<p><br><strong>Do you think silence still exists, or are we only left with various forms&nbsp;of filtered noise?</strong><br>The sound of the sea can, for example, be like silence to me, but when I close&nbsp;my eyes and imagine the sound of a highway, they actually sound quite&nbsp;similar to me. I consider the relationship between noise and silence quite&nbsp;ambivalent. Since I perceive silence more as a state of mind, it continues&nbsp;to exist, though it may become increasingly difficult to find &ndash; especially in&nbsp;places where fainter sounds are gradually being drowned out by louder ones.</p>
<p><strong>Your graduation EP explores the theme of overload while also seeking to&nbsp;create space for more sensitive listening and empathy. Isn&rsquo;t that a certain paradox &ndash; working with noise and intensity in order to help people&nbsp;perceive the subtleties and nuances of the soundscape once again?</strong><br>I think we can begin our search for subtlety within noise itself, which contains&nbsp;many layers and details. Noise can become a melody or a rhythm, it may begin to convey a certain feeling. We can experience the power of silence even&nbsp;through noise and intensity &ndash; especially in its absence or in contrast to it.</p>
